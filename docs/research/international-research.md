# Reading support beyond English: an international review for Læsehjælper

*Second literature review for Læsehjælper, as of 5 November 2026. It builds on the first review, [`dyslexia-reading-support.md`](dyslexia-reading-support.md), and does not repeat it. Research notes only: nothing here changes the README or the code. Items marked **Design inference** are suggestions for the app, not findings of the studies. Danish, Norwegian, Swedish and Icelandic research, most Finnish work and the Nordic agencies (SBU, VIVE) are covered by a separate review. Finnish studies appear here only where they are central to the international picture.*

Evidence levels (as in the first review):

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **L1** | Meta-analysis or systematic review |
| **L2** | Preregistered or large randomised controlled trial (RCT) |
| **L3** | Smaller RCT, or a randomised or matched trial with clear limitations |
| **L4** | Quasi-experiment, within-subject lab or eye-tracking study |
| **L5** | Correlational or cross-sectional work, model papers, reports, theses, opinion |

Reading marks, used for every source in section 9: **[full]** relevant parts of the full text read; **[abstract]** abstract only; **[abstract, via search]** abstract seen only as quoted by a search engine or repository page; **[metadata]** bibliographic record only; **[grey]** report, thesis, conference abstract, guidance or other non-peer-reviewed work. Grey literature is never the only basis for a claim that something works.

## 1. Summary

### For parents, in plain language

- **The basics are the same in every language.** Across German, French, Italian, Dutch, Spanish and English studies, what helps most is systematic teaching of letters, sounds and word parts, plus a lot of reading aloud with an adult who listens. Treatment effects for dyslexia are modest everywhere, about a third of a standard deviation on average, and published studies probably overstate them (Toffalini et al. 2021).
- **Coloured syllables: a small help for some beginners, for a while.** New German data are mixed. In one class study, second graders who practised with syllable arcs under part of their texts gained a little more fluency. Other German and South Tyrolean data found almost no average difference. Some children read worse with colours, and the small advantage at the end of first grade turned into a small disadvantage by the end of second grade. Use colours mainly in grades 1–2, mainly for long words, and plan to switch them off.
- **Every "extra cue" works this way.** Vowel points in Hebrew, short-vowel marks in Arabic, colours or spaces between words in Chinese and hyphens between syllables in Finnish all help beginners or unfamiliar words. They matter less as reading improves, and some start to slow readers down. Children with dyslexia do not always gain from them. Supports are meant to be removed step by step.
- **Highlighting each word while the page is read aloud does not seem to help learning.** In a French study, children remembered new words just as well without the moving highlight, and remembered their meanings slightly worse with it. Most children preferred no highlighting, although the weakest readers liked it.
- **"Listen first, then read it yourself" is right, but progress is slow.** A large German classroom trial of paired reading aloud found small gains: a little in fluency, somewhat more in understanding. The fluency gain had faded four months later. Gains on practised words do not fully carry over to new words. Keep going, and read new pages too.
- **Hearing a hard word on request helps.** Older computer studies found that letting children hear difficult words while they practise reading roughly doubled their gains compared with ordinary practice. Hearing the whole word and hearing it in parts worked about equally well. When sounds are joined, keep them joined smoothly rather than chopped apart.
- **When your child reads to you: pause, prompt, praise.** Let your child try. If they are stuck, give a hint rather than the answer, and praise effort. Ask a mix of open and closed questions, and ask your child to sum up and to guess what happens next (Education Endowment Foundation advice).
- **Apps alone do little.** Large French and English trials of phonics apps found small gains in letter-sound skills that often did not show up in later school reading tests. Help from an adult in a small group works better than computer programmes. Use the app with your child.
- **Every child is different, so try things out.** Adults read up to a third faster in their best font than in their worst, and what they liked did not predict what worked. Children's preferences for syllable colours vary just as much. Compare your child's reading with and without a setting before you decide.

## 2. Findings by language area

### 2.1 German-speaking countries (Germany, Austria, Switzerland, South Tyrol)

#### Syllable method, Silbenschrift, coloured-syllable primers

**Context.** Primers using the "Silbenmethode", where syllables alternate blue and red (e.g. *ABC der Tiere*, Mildenberger), are widespread in German schools, and children's publishers now sell "Silbengeschichten" in the same format. According to a search summary of a 2023 press release (not read in full), the publisher claims clearly better results for the method but cites no study. No independent evaluation of *ABC der Tiere* or of the Silbenfibel concept as a whole was found (web search in German; peDOCS; OpenAlex). The German Society for Reading and Writing (DGLS) issued a critical statement on the new syllable primers (Röber & Olfert 2010). That statement was not read here.

**Brügelmann 2025** (both versions, **[full] [grey]**). The English 3-page version and the German 11-page version were both read in full this time.
- **The author's own pilot.** 21 particularly weak readers in the middle of grade 2 read short, simple texts with or without blue/red syllable colouring. Silent reading speed was 93.7 vs 89.6 letters per minute, faster *without* colour. Grapheme errors were 3.2 % vs 3.9 % and comprehension 2.7 vs 2.5 points, both slightly better without colour. Word-level errors were nearly equal. Only the children's need for help was lower with colour (word level 1.9 % vs 4.7 %), with effect sizes above 0.5. Different children read different texts in the two formats (21 vs 13 reading samples), 16 of the 21 had learned to read with a syllable primer, and the subgroups were very small. The author concludes that there is no convincing advantage for colour on short, simple words.
- **A master's thesis.** Dannert 2022 (University of Bremen, not read) compared syllable colouring with the morpheme-marking mid-dot (*Mediopunkt*) for long words in the same group. Some children gained from one, some from the other, and some from neither.
- **Unpublished data reported second-hand:**
  - The Kaiserslautern group (Lachmann, Bergström) found a benefit in the middle of grade 1, no difference at the start of grade 2, and a benefit again at the end of grade 3.
  - Haase (Göttingen) found no benefit for beginners.
  - The SILLE study (Nickel, German-speaking schools in South Tyrol): no difference in mid-grade 1, a slight advantage at the end of grade 1 and a slight disadvantage at the end of grade 2, with effect sizes around 0.1. Effects varied widely between children, and for the same child between texts. A further 2025 data set found no reading-speed advantage in grades 1–3.
- **Conclusions.** Brügelmann recommends using colour only for some children, during the transition from sounding out to fluent word reading, and mostly for words of three or more syllables. He also argues that keeping colour into grades 3–4 hinders fluent reading.

**Dreschinski 2022, *Lesenlernen mit Silbenbögen*** (Heidelberg University of Education dissertation, published open access; **[full]** for method, results and conclusions; **[grey]**). This is the only German controlled study of a syllable display over months that was found.
- **Design.** Second-grade classes in two school districts, 2014–2016. 18 classes were recruited and 15 analysed (n = 221: 110 intervention, 111 control). Teachers volunteered. The author aimed for random assignment but could not achieve it, so this is a quasi-experiment. Classes that had used a syllable primer in grade 1 were excluded.
- **Intervention.** Intervention teachers drew syllable arcs (*Silbenbögen*) under an agreed share of the practice texts for several months. Control classes read the same kinds of texts without arcs.
- **Fluency result.** On a fluency composite (ELFE word and sentence comprehension, SLRT-II word reading), the intervention classes gained more: group × time F(1, 213) = 20.06, partial η² = .086, d = 0.35 (Morris). The analysis was at child level and ignored class clustering, so the true uncertainty is larger than reported.
- **Other results.**
  - Initial reading level did *not* moderate the effect: weak readers did not gain more, contrary to the author's hypothesis.
  - The arcs did not improve reading of syllable-joint spellings (*küssen, Teller*) or of reduced syllables, so they did not appear to teach syllable patterns.
- **Author's interpretation.** Arcs may ease "associative guessing" and working memory for long or unfamiliar words. They may also help children grasp morphological structure. Learning to read, however, means building word-specific spellings in memory, with or without arcs. L4.

**Ongoing work.** The Kaiserslautern/DFKI project (Gauditz et al. 2025, workshop abstract, **[grey]**) is an eye-tracking study of grades 1–4 reading with and without coloured syllables, longitudinal in grade 1. No results are public. A University of Erfurt project (from 2025) tests visual marking of word stress for beginners; no results yet. Neither can support claims yet.

**Syllable-based *training* (not display).** Beyond Müller et al. 2017 and 2020 (first review):
- Müller, Karageorgos & Richter 2021 (**[abstract]**, L3) replicated the *Willy Wortbär* training: 101 below-average second graders were randomised to 24 small-group sessions of repeated reading and segmentation of frequent syllables, or to a waiting group. Word recognition became faster and more accurate.
- Heß et al. 2024 (**[abstract]**, L3) turned the training into a game-based app. 132 low-skilled second graders were randomly assigned to 20 sessions or a waiting group. Word recognition and phonological recoding improved. Orthographic decoding improved only in less impaired children, and reading comprehension did not change.
- These studies support syllable *practice* for weak second graders. They do not test colouring text.

**When German children use syllables and morphemes.** In a lexical-decision study, syllables were the preferred sub-word unit for second graders. Fourth graders also used morphemes when the display emphasised them, and adults read more holistically (Hasenäcker & Schroeder 2017, **[abstract]**, L4). This fits a stage model in which syllable cues suit grade 2 and word-part cues suit older or more skilled readers.

**Compound segmentation in German "Leichte Sprache".** German Easy Language rules split compounds with a hyphen or a mid-dot (*Mediopunkt*). Deilen, Hansen-Schirra & Nagels 2022 (**[abstract]**, L4, eye tracking) found:
- Segmentation helped pupils with low literacy (hearing-impaired pupils), but only when the boundary was marked subtly, close to the standard spelling.
- Pupils with higher literacy and unimpaired adults did not gain.
- Hyphens gave skilled readers quick access to the first part of the compound but slowed the word as a whole.

This is the closest German evidence on marking word parts.

#### Fluency methods: Lautlese-Tandems and related work

**Lauer-Schmaltz, Rosebrock & Gold 2014** (*Didaktik Deutsch*, **[full]**, L3) is the main German classroom trial of reading-aloud methods with primary-school children.
- **Sample.** 45 grade-3 classes from 18 schools in the Rhine-Main area (n = 893 at pretest, mean age 8;8). 54 % of the children came from homes where another language was spoken.
- **Assignment.** Classes were assigned by lot to *Lautlesetandems* (a stronger and a weaker reader read aloud together, 12 classes), class-wide choral reading with the teacher as model (16 classes), or control (17 classes). In some cases teachers' preferences were taken into account.
- **Dose.** Three times a week, 15–20 minutes, for half a school year.
- **Results (latent change models).**
  - Fluency gains over control right after the programme were significant but small: d<sub>corr</sub> = 0.13 for tandems and 0.09 for choral reading.
  - Four months later, only choral reading still differed from control (d<sub>corr</sub> = 0.12). The tandem difference was no longer significant (p = .08).
  - Comprehension gains were larger right after the programme (tandems d<sub>corr</sub> = 0.28; choral reading 0.12) but did not differ from control at follow-up.
  - In tandems, children with higher initial fluency gained more (β = .23). Once initial level was controlled, the tandem advantage was no longer significant.
- **Contrast with older readers.** The authors contrast these results with medium effects of the same method in weak-reading 12-year-olds (Rosebrock et al. 2010, not read).

**Austrian fluency research.**
- Thaler, Ebner, Wimmer & Landerl 2004 (**[metadata]**; the finding is stated in the title) found word-specific effects but little transfer to untrained words in dysfluent but accurate readers.
- Hintikka, Landerl, Aro & Lyytinen 2008 (**[abstract, via search]**) found that computer-training gains were specific to the trained material and did not raise general reading speed.
- Huemer, Landerl, Aro & Lyytinen 2008 (**[abstract, via search]**, 39 German-speaking poor readers in grades 2 and 4) compared two speed-training programmes. One gave faster gains in global word-reading fluency than the computer programme, and neither improved pseudoword reading.
- German-speaking dyslexia is mainly a problem of reading speed, which Austrian studies have found hard to move. All L3/L4, small samples.

#### Programmes and the German LRS research groups

- **Ise, Engel & Schulte-Körne 2012** (*Kindheit und Entwicklung*, **[abstract]**, L1) meta-analysed German-language programmes for reading and spelling disorder.
  - Symptom-specific programmes, which teach reading and spelling directly, were clearly more effective than training of underlying functions or perception.
  - Effectiveness depended on who delivered the programme and on its intensity, not on age or severity.
  - The S3 guideline's evidence table, quoted via a search result, adds that programmes longer than 20 weeks had about twice the effect (g = 0.82). That number was not checked against the paper.
  - The *Kieler Leseaufbau*, the *Lautgetreue Lese-Rechtschreibförderung* and the *Marburger Rechtschreibtraining* belong to the symptom-specific family. No independent RCT of the first two was found. The Marburger programme targets spelling, which is outside the app's scope.
- **German S3 guideline.** The 2015 version (Galuschka & Schulte-Körne 2016, first review) is listed as expired in the AWMF register. An update was registered in 2020. No updated version was found as of November 2026 (web search of the AWMF register and LMU pages, **[grey]**). The 2015 recommendations therefore remain the latest.
- **The Galuschka, Schulte-Körne, Moll, Landerl, Richter and Müller groups.** Their key syntheses are already in the first review (Galuschka 2014, 2020; S3) or above (Müller, Richter). De Simone, Moll et al. 2023 (first review) found that colour alternation did not change adult eye movements.
- **Austrian morpheme training.** *MORPHEUS* (Kargl & Purgstaller, Graz) teaches reading and spelling via morphemes. Only small, developer-involved studies were found, and they are not used here.
- **Switzerland.** No Swiss effectiveness trial of syllable display, fluency methods or reading apps was found. Swiss contributions identified were mainly neuroimaging, for example a study of brain changes in children learning letter–sound correspondences (Brem et al. 2010, **[metadata]**).

**What it could mean for Læsehjælper (design inference):** the German evidence fits an app that offers syllable cues to beginners in grades 1–2, mainly for long words, and makes switching them off easy. It also fits subtle rather than salient compound marking for older or weaker readers, and a "listen, then read" flow whose effects are expected to be small and to depend on regular use.

### 2.2 Dutch and Flemish

- **Spacing and fonts.** The Dutch studies are already in the first review: Hakvoort et al. 2017, van den Boer & Hakvoort 2015, Kuster et al. 2018 and Marinus et al. 2016. Nothing newer was found.
- **Computer-based training.**
  - Van Gorp, Segers & Verhoeven 2016/2017 (*Reading Research Quarterly*, **[abstract, via search]**, L3): 62 poor-reading Dutch second graders played a word-identification game on a tablet for five hours over five weeks, compared with a waiting control. Decoding efficiency rose on untrained words and pseudowords on all six lists. The game used repetition, immediate corrective feedback and a meaning task.
  - Vanden Bempt et al. 2021 (Flanders, **[abstract, via search]**, L3): 88 kindergartners at cognitive risk for dyslexia played GraphoGame-Flemish for 12 weeks, compared with an active control game and no game. Letter knowledge and word decoding improved, with no transfer to untrained phonological or language skills. Long-term effects are unknown.
  - Glatz et al. 2023 is in the first review.
- **Dutch dyslexia protocol.** Dyslexia care in the Netherlands (the protocol for severe dyslexia) is evaluated mainly through clinical cohort data. No RCT of protocol components was found. Cohort studies exist (e.g. Tilanus, Segers & Verhoeven; an online-versus-face-to-face comparison in 2023) but were not read and are not used.

**Design inference:** short, frequent practice with immediate feedback transferred to untrained words in the van Gorp study. This supports the tap-to-hear feedback loop more than any display feature.

### 2.3 French

**Dehaene's group: Kalulu and ELAN.**
- **Potier Watkins et al. 2020** (*Journal of Computers in Education*, **[abstract]**, L2) tested the ELAN app: explicit, systematic grapheme–phoneme teaching with fully decodable text.
  - Design: randomised trial in 44 first-grade classes (n = 975).
  - Children who used the app in the first term improved more than those using maths software or no tablet, in word and pseudoword reading fluency and in sentence comprehension.
- **Potier Watkins & Dehaene 2024** (*Journal of Experimental Education*, online 2023, **[abstract]**, L2) moved the intervention to kindergarten.
  - Design: 40 classes (n = 1,092) were randomised to the Kalulu phonics game or a matched number game, then crossed over at mid-year.
  - Phonics players improved in letter naming, grapheme–phoneme matching and reading fluency.
  - At the start of grade 1 they still had an advantage in phoneme awareness and grapheme–phoneme matching, but it did *not* translate into school literacy gains by mid-grade 1.
  - The earlier first-grade version helped only when it was used alongside reading instruction at the start of the year.

**GraphoGame in French.** Lassault et al. 2022 (*Scientific Studies of Reading*, **[abstract, via search]**, L3):
- **Design.** 921 socioeconomically disadvantaged primary-school children used GraphoGame or an equally engaging maths game (Fiete Math) four times a week for 30 minutes over about four months. The analysis used mixed models with classes as random effects. Allocation was by cohort and is not clearly an individual randomisation.
- **Results.** GraphoGame improved phoneme awareness (ES 0.23), orthographic choice (0.27) and word-reading fluency (0.18).

**Lalilo.** A company slide deck (**[grey]**) mentions a 2024–25 RCT with Sciences Po and Paris-Dauphine, with about 3,000 pupils. No results were found. Gap.

**Syllable-based work.**
- Ecalle, Kleinsz & Magnan 2013 (**[abstract, via search]**, L3/L4) compared grapho-syllabic and grapho-phonemic computer training in poor readers in grade 2 (n = 27) and grade 1 (n = 18). The syllable group gained most in word reading, and the grade-1 sample was followed for 16 months.
- Doignon-Camus & Zagar 2014 (**[abstract]**, L4) showed that pre-readers quickly learn links between letter clusters and spoken syllables (a "syllabic bridge"), especially with explicit instruction.
- Sargiani et al. 2018 (Portuguese, section 2.5) point the other way for the very first steps.

**Colour coding: LireCouleur, coloured graphemes, grey silent letters.** LireCouleur colours syllables or sounds and can set silent letters in a special style.
- No peer-reviewed evaluation of LireCouleur, of coloured graphemes (*graphèmes colorés*) or of greying silent letters was found. The search covered the HAL API (title, abstract and full-text queries), DUMAS and web search in French and English.
- Only student theses exist. These are teacher-training and speech-therapy *mémoires*, such as one on syllable and vowel colouring with dyslexic CE1–CE2 pupils. The repository blocked access, so they were not read and are not used.
- This is a clear gap.

**Synchronised highlighting.** Gerbier, Bailly & Bosse 2018 (**[abstract]** via HAL, L4) is the most direct test of "read-aloud with word highlighting".
- **Design.** 40 French children in grades 3–5 read stories while hearing them, either in plain black text or with karaoke-style highlighting (grey text turning black as each word was spoken). The stories contained pseudowords, and memory for them was tested without warning.
- **Results.** Highlighting had no effect on memory for the pseudowords' spelling and a *negative* effect on memory for their meaning category. Eye tracking showed fewer but longer fixations and fewer regressions with highlighting: the highlight pulled children along with the voice. Most children preferred the plain version, while the weakest readers tended to prefer highlighting.

**French guidance (INSERM 2007, HAS 2018).** See section 4.

### 2.4 Italian

- **Spacing.** Zorzi et al. 2012 and Galliussi et al. 2020 are in the first review. No newer Italian child studies of spacing were found.
- **Fluency in a transparent orthography.** In Italian, dyslexia is usually described as mainly a problem of reading speed, which is why Italian treatments target fluency. The Zoccolotti group's work on this was found only through secondary summaries and is not cited directly.
  - Tressoldi, Vio & Iozzino 2007 (**[abstract, via search]**, L4, 63 dyslexic children): a sub-syllabic method with automatic, timed presentation of target syllables improved fluency more than the comparison method. Fluency rose at about the same rate with each repetition of the treatment.
- **Morphemes as reading units.** Burani, Marcolini, De Luca & Zoccolotti 2008 (*Cognition*, **[abstract, via search]**, L4):
  - All groups read root-plus-suffix pseudowords faster and more accurately than simple pseudowords.
  - Dyslexic and younger children, but not skilled readers, also gained from morphological structure in real words.
  - Morphemes thus act as a mid-sized unit for readers with weak whole-word skills. This is evidence for the *idea* behind word-part display, although nothing was marked visually.
- **Treatment research quality.** Toffalini et al. 2021 (*Behavior Research Methods*, **[abstract]**, L1) reviewed dyslexia treatment studies.
  - The mean effect was d = 0.38 [0.31, 0.46].
  - Samples were small, multiple testing was common, and the risk of publication bias was high, so many significant results probably overestimate effects.
  - This is a useful caution for every small positive study in this review.
- **Colour.** Pinna & Deiana 2018 (*i-Perception*, **[abstract]**, L4) coloured text by word, half-word, syllable or letter, or kept it one colour. Reading time, errors and comprehension changed with the condition, with similar patterns in typical and dyslexic children and adults. The abstract does not say which condition helped or hurt, and the full text could not be retrieved, so it cannot be used to argue either way.
- **National guideline.** The Istituto Superiore di Sanità published an updated guideline on specific learning disorders in 2022 (**[metadata]**). Its treatment chapters could not be read.

### 2.5 Spanish and Portuguese

- **Spacing.** Perea, Panadero, Moret-Tatay & Gómez 2012 (*Learning and Instruction*, **[abstract, via search]**, L4) is a Spanish lexical-decision study. Wider letter spacing shortened word-identification times in young typical readers and more so in children with dyslexia. It used isolated words, not text.
- **Type size.** Rello et al. 2013 (**[abstract]**, L4, people with dyslexia reading Wikipedia articles; a Spanish research group) found that font size affected readability and comprehension and line spacing did not. Rello, Pielot & Marcos 2016 (**[abstract]**, L4, 104 participants, 10–26 pt) found that readability improved with size and that comprehension was better at 18 and 26 pt. Neither sample was described as young children.
- **Syllable highlighting.** Lopes & Barrera 2019 (Brazil) is in the first review. No Spanish or European-Portuguese study of syllable colouring was found.
- **Syllable-based teaching.**
  - Giménez et al. 2021 (*Journal of Computer Assisted Learning*, **[abstract, via search]**, L3): a stratified randomised trial with 32 at-risk Spanish first graders compared a computer-assisted programme centred on syllable decoding (four 15-minute sessions a week plus a group comprehension session, 11 weeks) with the standard school support service. It reported better decoding and comprehension, with moderate to large effects. The sample was very small.
  - Sargiani, Ehri & Maluf 2018 (*Applied Psycholinguistics*, **[abstract]**, L3, Brazilian kindergartners, n = 90) point the other way:
    - Teaching phoneme-level mapping with letters and articulation beat syllable-level mapping and no training for phoneme segmentation, reading and spelling.
    - Only the phoneme-plus-articulation group remembered how to segment phonemes 1.5 years later.
    - The authors conclude that phonemic mapping prepares children better than syllabic mapping, even though syllables are more salient in spoken Portuguese.

**Design inference:** syllables are a good bridge to larger units *after* letter–sound knowledge is in place, not a substitute for it. Læsehjælper's tap-to-hear sequence (whole → syllables → whole) is a support for children who already decode, not a first-steps method.

### 2.6 Hebrew and Arabic: vowel diacritics as an added cue

Hebrew is first taught with *niqqud* (vowel points) and later read without them. Arabic children start with fully vowelised text (*harakat*, *tashkeel*), which is later dropped.

- **Hebrew, typical readers.** Schiff 2012 (*Journal of Psycholinguistic Research*, **[abstract, via search]**, L5, cross-sectional, 126 children in grades 2, 4 and 6):
  - Second graders read and understood pointed text more accurately and faster than unpointed text.
  - In grades 4 and 6, reading of unpointed text developed further than reading of pointed text.
  - Accuracy on pointed words mediated unpointed reading in grade 2, but no longer in grade 6.
- **Hebrew, dyslexic readers.** Schiff, Katzir & Shoshan 2013 (*Annals of Dyslexia*, **[abstract, via search]**, L4/L5):
  - Fourth graders with dyslexia read pointed and unpointed words with *no* difference in accuracy or speed.
  - Their speed matched typical second graders, but their accuracy on pointed words was lower.
  - The authors' title calls it "the road not taken": the dyslexic children had neither profited from the cue nor developed the more efficient strategy.
- **The transition.** Share & Bar-On 2018 (*Journal of Learning Disabilities*, **[abstract, via search]**, L5, model paper) describe Hebrew reading development in three stages:
  - sound-by-sound decoding in grade 1;
  - larger, lexical units in grade 2;
  - context-based reading of homograph-rich unpointed text in the upper grades.
  
  They treat the move from pointed to unpointed text as a major challenge in its own right.
- **Arabic.** Asadi 2017 (*Writing Systems Research*, **[abstract]**, L4) had 1,516 children in grades 1–6 read matched word lists with and without short-vowel marks. Fluency was *higher without* the marks in every grade. Accuracy was also higher without them, except in grades 1–2, where it was similar. The author concludes that the marks add visual complexity and density.

**What these literatures say:** vowel marks help beginners in the first one to two years, especially for accuracy. Their value falls or reverses as reading becomes lexical. Readers with dyslexia may not benefit even when they are still slow. Leaving them out at a planned point is a standard part of instruction, but no trial was found that compared *when* or *how* to drop them.

### 2.7 Chinese and Japanese: pinyin, furigana, word spacing and word colouring

Chinese is written without spaces between words, so marking word boundaries is a close analogue of marking syllables or word parts.

- **Spaces.**
  - Blythe et al. 2012 (*Journal of Memory and Language*, **[abstract, via search]**, L4): children aged 7–10 and adults learned new two-character words embedded in sentences, either with spaces between words or without. Spaced learners read the new words faster during learning. Children kept the advantage in a later *unspaced* test session, so the cue did not leave a dependence, at least in the short term.
  - Perea & Wang 2017 (**[full]**) summarise a broader picture: inserting spaces into familiar Chinese, Japanese or Thai text usually does not speed adult reading overall. In Zang et al. 2013, spaces shortened first fixations on words for adults and children, but the advantage disappeared in total reading time, presumably because spaced text looks unfamiliar.
- **Alternating word colours**, which mark boundaries without changing layout:
  - Perea & Wang 2017 (*Memory & Cognition*, **[full]**, L4): 24 typical Chinese second graders read a text aloud 5.6 % faster with alternating-colour words than with single-colour words (81.5 vs 77.2 words per minute; t(23) = 3.55, Bayes factor 22.7). Comprehension was at ceiling. Adults benefited only with technical, unfamiliar texts.
  - Pan, Liu, Li & Yan 2021 (*Reading and Writing*, **[abstract, via search]**, L4, third graders): eye-movement measures showed a benefit. The children with the *highest* word-segmentation ability gained most.
  - Song et al. 2021 (*Reading and Writing*, **[abstract, via search]**, L4, oral reading in grades 2–5): grades 2 and 3 read faster with alternating colours. A secondary summary reports no difference in grades 4 and 5.
  - Liao & Hsiao 2025 (*Learning and Instruction*, **[abstract]**, L4, 63 children in grades 2–3): children who changed their eye-movement pattern towards more sequential reading gained comprehension with colour markers, but not with spaces. The authors stress individual differences and the cost of disrupting habits a child has already learned.
- **Pinyin and furigana.** No peer-reviewed study with native-speaking children was found that tested whether pinyin over characters, or furigana over kanji, helps or hinders character learning, or creates dependence. Eye-tracking reports (secondary) show that beginners look at pinyin. A Japanese grant summary claims benefits from furigana but is not peer-reviewed and is not used. Japanese children are first taught with spaced hiragana, but no child eye-tracking study of this was found.

**What these literatures say:** a boundary cue helps most when the boundary is not yet automatic (grades 2–3, unfamiliar words). It helps less or not at all later. A cue that does not change spatial layout (colour) disturbs less than one that does (spaces). Children who already have some segmentation skill may gain most.

### 2.8 Finnish work central to the international picture

Finnish primers hyphenate words at syllable boundaries. Hyphens are dropped for two-syllable words in grade 2 but kept for longer words. This makes Finland a natural experiment in cue fading.

- Häikiö, Bertram & Hyönä 2016 (*Reading and Writing*, **[abstract, via repository]**, L4):
  - Hyphenated words took longer to read than unhyphenated ones for second graders.
  - First graders were also slowed on long words, even when the hyphen was made smaller.
  - The cost was larger for long words and for better readers.
- Häikiö & Luotojärvi 2022 (*Scientific Studies of Reading*, **[abstract]**, L4): even for *novel* words, where the cue should help most, syllable hyphenation gave no benefit in grades 1–2. The authors suggest it hinders the use of spelling patterns.
- Häikiö, Bertram & Hyönä 2011 (*Applied Psycholinguistics*, **[abstract, via search]**, L4): hyphenated compound words slowed most second, fourth and sixth graders, but *slow* second graders read them faster. This suggests that readers who still process word by word gain from a part boundary and others lose.
- GraphoGame is covered by McTigue et al. 2020 (first review) and, internationally, by the French and English trials in sections 2.3 and 4.

## 3. Added cues and fading

### 3.1 What the analogues have in common

| Cue | Language | Helps whom, when | Stops helping or costs | Level |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Syllable arcs | German | Grade 2 classes, small gain (d = 0.35); not more for weak readers | Did not teach syllable patterns | L4 grey (Dreschinski 2022) |
| Syllable colour | German, South Tyrol | Slight advantage end of grade 1; benefit in some children | Slight disadvantage end of grade 2; some children disturbed | L5 grey (Brügelmann 2025, SILLE) |
| Syllable colour | Finnish | No effect on speed | — | L4 (Häikiö 2015, first review) |
| Syllable hyphens | Finnish | Not even for novel words | Slows readers; more so for skilled readers and long words | L4 (Häikiö 2016, 2022) |
| Syllable highlight | Brazilian Portuguese | Weak second graders, isolated words | Not tested | L4 (Lopes & Barrera 2019) |
| Vowel points | Hebrew | Grade 2 accuracy and speed | Value falls by grades 4–6; dyslexic grade-4 readers do not gain | L4/L5 (Schiff 2012, 2013) |
| Short-vowel marks | Arabic | No gain even in grades 1–2 (similar accuracy) | Lower fluency in all grades; lower accuracy from grade 3 | L4 (Asadi 2017) |
| Word colours | Chinese | Grades 2–3; unfamiliar words for adults | No effect in grades 4–5 | L4 (Perea & Wang 2017; Pan 2021; Song 2021) |
| Word spaces | Chinese | Learning new words, ages 7–10; benefit kept without spaces | Overall reading not faster for familiar text | L4 (Blythe 2012; Zang 2013 via Perea & Wang) |
| Compound boundary | German (Easy Language), Finnish | Low-literacy pupils, slow second graders; subtle marking only | Skilled readers slowed; salient marking no help | L4 (Deilen 2022; Häikiö 2011) |
| Pictures next to words | English | — | Pictures compete with print and reduce sight-word learning | L4 (Torcasio & Sweller 2010) |
| Karaoke highlighting | French | Preferred by the weakest readers | No gain for word forms, loss for word meanings | L4 (Gerbier 2018) |

### 3.2 Findings

1. **Cues help when the unit they mark is not yet automatic.** This covers beginners (grades 1–3), long or unfamiliar words, and readers who still decode piece by piece (Perea & Wang 2017; Song 2021; Schiff 2012; Häikiö 2011; Deilen 2022). L4.
2. **The benefit shrinks with skill and can reverse** (Häikiö 2016; Asadi 2017; SILLE). This matches the *expertise-reversal effect* from cognitive-load research: guidance that helps novices becomes redundant for more expert learners and can hinder them (Kalyuga et al. 2003, L5 review of experiments).
3. **Children with dyslexia are not reliably the ones who benefit most.** Dyslexic Hebrew fourth graders did not gain from vowel points (Schiff 2013). Weak German second graders did not gain more from arcs than others (Dreschinski 2022). Chinese children with *better* segmentation skills gained most from colour (Pan 2021). L4.
4. **Subtle beats salient.** A boundary helps only when it stays close to the normal written form (Deilen 2022). Colour, which keeps the layout, disturbs less than spaces (Perea & Wang 2017; Liao & Hsiao 2025). Even a small hyphen slowed Finnish children (Häikiö 2016). L4.
5. **Evidence on dependence is thin and mixed.**
   - *Reassuring:* children who learned new words with spaces read them as well later without spaces (Blythe 2012).
   - *Cautionary:* added cues can draw attention away from the letters that must be learned (Torcasio & Sweller 2010; Häikiö & Luotojärvi 2022; Gerbier 2018). Dyslexic Hebrew readers stay stuck between strategies (Schiff 2013). The pointed-to-unpointed transition is a hurdle of its own (Share & Bar-On 2018). Arcs did not teach syllable patterns (Dreschinski 2022).
6. **Fading is standard practice but untested.** Finnish primers drop hyphens for two-syllable words in grade 2 (Häikiö 2016, background). Hebrew schooling moves to unpointed text across grades 2–4 (Schiff 2012). EEF guidance says support should be gradually reduced as the child progresses (EEF KS1 guidance, L5). **No trial was found** that compares constant with faded reading cues, or different fading schedules. The historical British *Initial Teaching Alphabet* experiments of the 1960s are often cited on the transition problem, but their primary reports could not be accessed, so they are not used here.

### 3.3 What it could mean for Læsehjælper (design inferences)

- **Stage-based defaults:** syllable cues for profiles of beginners (grades 1–2), word-part cues for older or more advanced readers. This follows Hasenäcker & Schroeder 2017 and the Hebrew and Chinese stage pattern.
- **Long words only:** limit syllable colouring to words of three or more syllables, as the Finnish practice and Brügelmann recommend.
- **Planned fading:** a gentle prompt after some weeks of use, or once the child reads a page fluently, to try the next page without the cue, and to switch the cue off if reading is no worse.
- **Subtle styles:** prefer colour or a fine mid-dot over hyphens or gaps for word parts.
- **"Cue-off check":** compare one page with and without the cue, because preference and benefit differ between children and within a child over time.

## 4. International evidence bodies

### 4.1 What Works Clearinghouse (US Institute of Education Sciences)

- **Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding in Kindergarten Through 3rd Grade** (Foorman et al. 2016, revised 2019; **[grey]**, summary page read). Recommendations and evidence levels:
  1. Teach academic language (minimal).
  2. Develop awareness of speech sounds and their links to letters (strong).
  3. Teach students to decode words, analyse word parts, and write and recognise words (strong).
  4. Make sure each student reads connected text every day to support accuracy, fluency and comprehension (moderate).
  
  *For the app:* daily connected-text reading with an adult (the "your turn" step) matches recommendation 4. Word-part display touches recommendation 3, but the guide recommends *teaching* word parts, not marking them.
- **Providing Reading Interventions for Students in Grades 4–9** (Vaughn et al. 2022; **[grey]**, overview read). Recommendations:
  1. Build decoding of complex multisyllabic words (strong).
  2. Purposeful fluency-building activities (strong), including giving each repeated reading a purpose.
  3. Comprehension-building practices (strong).
  4. Practice with "stretch" text (moderate).
  
  This is relevant to older dyslexic users. Multisyllabic decoding is where syllable and word-part support makes most sense.
- **Intervention report: Repeated Reading, students with learning disabilities** (May 2014; **[grey]**, relevant pages read).
  - Of 194 studies identified, two met standards (78 students, grades 5–12).
  - Results: potentially positive effects on comprehension (improvement index +7), and no discernible effects on fluency (−1, range −4 to +5), alphabetics or general reading.
  - The extent of evidence is rated small.
  - This tempers the large effects in Lee & Yoon 2017 (first review), which come mostly from small studies measured on practised passages.

### 4.2 Education Endowment Foundation (UK)

- **Improving Literacy in Key Stage 1** (ages 5–7, 2nd edition 2020; **[grey]**, recommendations poster and relevant pages read).
  - It makes eight recommendations, including a systematic phonics programme, a balanced approach to decoding and comprehension, and structured interventions for struggling pupils.
  - On fluency, it says children need a large amount of purposeful practice with feedback, and texts they can decode with the letter–sound patterns already taught.
  - On support, it says support should be reduced gradually until the child can work independently.
- **Improving Literacy in Key Stage 2** (ages 7–11; **[grey]**, recommendation text seen only in search excerpts). Recommendation 2 is to develop fluent reading through guided oral reading, where the teacher models fluent reading and pupils read the same text aloud with feedback, and through repeated reading of short passages.
- **Working with Parents to Support Children's Learning** (van Poortvliet, Axford & Lloyd 2018; **[grey]**, relevant pages read).
  - Shared reading should be central for young children, and the parent–child talk during it is thought to be the key ingredient.
  - As children get older, parents should listen to them read using "pause, prompt, praise".
  - Questions should mix open and closed forms (who, what, where, when, why), and children should be asked to summarise and predict.
  - Book-gifting alone is unlikely to be effective.
  - The report also says that, for academic outcomes, classroom interventions have more evidence than parenting interventions.
- **EEF trials of technology-based reading programmes:**
  - **GraphoGame Rime** (Worth et al. 2018, NFER; **[grey] [full]** executive summary): pupil-randomised, 398 Year-2 pupils with weak phonics in 15 schools. Effect on reading −0.06 [−0.23, 0.12], about −1 month, with very high security. Pupils from low-income families: −0.01 [−0.43, 0.42].
  - **Lexia Reading Core5** (Year 2, 57 schools, 697 pupils; **[grey]**, EEF summary seen only via search): about +1 month, revised down from +2, with high security, and no effect on the national KS1 reading test.
  - **ABRA** (Year 1; **[grey]**, via search): about +3 months, with larger effects for the pen-and-paper version than for the online version.
- **Teaching and Learning Toolkit.** The former "digital technology" strand (about +4 months on average, with wide variation) was later folded into the strands for specific approaches (**[grey]**, via search; date of the change not checked).

### 4.3 Australian Education Research Organisation (AERO)

- *Introduction to the science of reading* (2023; **[grey]**, via search) uses the Simple View of Reading and stresses that fluency needs deliberate practice.
- AERO's evidence snapshot on tier 2 and tier 3 support (**[grey]**, via search) recommends:
  - repeated reading of 50–200-word passages aloud to a teacher, with corrective feedback;
  - systematic synthetic phonics as the first step for older students who still cannot decode.
- No AERO guidance on display aids (colour, spacing, read-aloud tools) was found.

### 4.4 Cochrane and Campbell

- **Dietrichson et al. 2021**, Campbell Systematic Reviews (**[abstract]**, L1; full text open):
  - Scope: targeted school-based interventions for K–6 students with or at risk of academic difficulties. 205 studies were included in a meta-analysis, 93 % of interventions were RCTs, 86 % came from the US, and the mean grade was 2.4.
  - Average effects: 0.30 [0.25, 0.34] short-term and 0.27 [0.17, 0.36] at follow-up.
  - Peer-assisted instruction and adult small-group instruction had effects of about 0.35–0.45, significantly larger than computer-assisted instruction, coaching of staff, incentives and progress monitoring.
  - The review is led by VIVE (Denmark), so it overlaps with the Nordic review.
- **Cochrane.** No Cochrane or Campbell review of display aids (spacing, colour coding, text-to-speech, highlighting) for children was found. McArthur et al. 2018 (phonics, Cochrane) is in the first review.
- **German-language meta-analysis.** Ise et al. 2012 (section 2.1) plays a similar role for German.
- **Treatment-research quality.** Toffalini et al. 2021 (section 2.4).

### 4.5 National guidelines

- **Germany: S3 guideline (2015).** Still the latest version (section 2.1).
- **France: INSERM collective expertise, *Dyslexie, dysorthographie, dyscalculie* (2007;** **[grey]**, synthesis read in full). Key points:
  - Phonological training combined with decoding, done intensively and explicitly in small groups, helps children with decoding difficulties.
  - Decoding training improves reading *speed* little. The method most recognised for fluency is repeated reading of letters, words and sentences until a target speed is reached. Studies cited used about six minutes a day for six to nine months.
  - Computer tools deserve rigorous evaluation and can only complement, not replace, a teacher.
  - A French syllable-based audiovisual program showed "some" efficacy but needed stronger evaluation.
  - Effective programmes needed about 4–5 sessions a week.
  - Coloured glasses, prisms and covering one eye still lacked solid validation.
- **France: Haute Autorité de Santé, *Comment améliorer le parcours de santé d'un enfant avec troubles spécifiques du langage et des apprentissages ?*** (synthesis 2018; **[grey]**, read in full). This is a guide to a graded, coordinated care pathway (school, primary care, specialists, reference centres). It does not rate specific reading aids.
- **Italy: Istituto Superiore di Sanità guideline on specific learning disorders (2022;** **[metadata]**, not read).

**What it could mean for Læsehjælper (design inference):** none of the evidence bodies endorses display aids. All of them endorse:
- daily reading of connected text with feedback;
- fluency practice with a model;
- multisyllabic decoding for older strugglers;
- adult-led, not app-led, support.

The app's strongest evidence-based role is to make adult-supported reading of real books easier and more frequent.

## 6. Issue candidates

*These are design inferences strong enough, in this reviewer's judgement, to justify a change. Each lists its evidence level. None rests on grey literature alone.*

1. **Offer "long words only" for syllable colours and arcs, and use it as the default for new profiles.** Evidence:
   - Finnish practice and data: hyphens cost more on long words and for skilled readers (Häikiö 2016, L4).
   - Chinese colour analogue: benefits in grades 2–3, none in grades 4–5 (L4).
   - Dreschinski 2022 and Brügelmann 2025 (L4/L5, grey).

   Level: **L4 (converging, no RCT).** The first review proposed off by default. This review finds a small early benefit plausible, so "long words only" is a defensible middle ground. Either way it needs an easy switch per profile.
2. **Add a fading prompt (a "cue-off check").** After some weeks of use, or once a page is read fluently, suggest that the adult try the next page without colours or arcs, and switch them off if reading is no worse. Evidence:
   - the cue-value-falls-with-skill pattern across German, Finnish, Hebrew, Arabic and Chinese (L4);
   - the expertise-reversal effect (L5 review);
   - EEF guidance to reduce support gradually (L5).

   Level: **L4/L5.** No trial of fading schedules exists, so wording should stay advisory.
3. **Make word-by-word highlighting during read-aloud a per-profile setting.** Evidence:
   - Gerbier et al. 2018: no gain for word forms, loss for word meanings, children mostly preferred no highlighting (L4).
   - Keelor et al. 2023: no comprehension gain (L4, first review).
   - Clinton-Lisell 2023: self-paced reading-while-listening ≈ 0 (L1, first review).

   Level: **L4.** Under AGENTS.md ("features without clear evidence are optional"), highlighting should at least be switchable. The weakest readers' preference for it argues for keeping it available.
4. **Mark word parts subtly and only on long compound words.** Use a slight gap, a fine mid-dot or a colour shift rather than hyphens, and apply it only to long compounds, not every word. Evidence: Deilen et al. 2022 and Häikiö et al. 2011 (L4); Hasenäcker & Schroeder 2017 (L4). Level: **L4.** The muting of inflectional endings has no evidence either way. Keep it, but describe it as untested in the README.
5. **Show "how to listen" tips for the adult in "your turn" and "read together".** Tips: pause, prompt, praise; a few question stems (who, what, where, why; "what happened?", "what will happen next?"). Evidence:
   - EEF parental-engagement guidance (L5, based on reviews);
   - Sénéchal & Young 2008, parents tutoring and listening beat reading to the child (L1, first review);
   - Andersen & Nielsen 2016 (L2, first review);
   - Flack et al. 2018 (L1).

   Level: **L1–L2 for the principle, L5 for the exact wording.** This firms up the first review's inference 3.5.
6. **Keep the syllables in tap-to-hear audio connected.** Use short or no pauses between syllables, keep ending with the whole word, and perhaps offer a "hint first" mode that plays the syllables before the whole word. Evidence: Gonzalez-Frey & Ehri 2021, for phonemes, not syllables (L3/L4); van Daal & Reitsma 1990 (L4); EEF "prompt, not the answer" (L5). Level: **L4, indirect.** Lower priority.

Not proposed as issues: spacing, fonts and font size, where nothing new changes the first review's recommendations.

## 7. Gaps

- **No RCT of any syllable-colour or syllable-arc display, in any language.** The German work is unpublished (Kaiserslautern, Göttingen, SILLE) or quasi-experimental (Dreschinski). No preregistered study exists.
- **Danish:** no study of syllable or word-part display. No study anywhere of *muting* pronounced inflectional endings. A plausible risk is that children skip endings they already misread.
- **French colour tools:** no evaluation of LireCouleur, coloured graphemes or greyed silent letters.
- **Fading:** no trial comparing constant and faded reading cues, or different fading schedules, in any orthography.
- **Pinyin and furigana:** no study of dependence or transfer with native-speaking children was found.
- **Highlighting** has been studied only in children of 8 and older (Gerbier; Keelor). Nothing covers 5–7-year-olds learning to decode, where highlighting might support finger-pointing and the voice–print link.
- **Tap-to-hear** evidence is mostly from before 2000, from small samples, and on computers. There is no modern study of on-demand word audio in e-books for beginners, and no test of the order whole → parts → whole.
- **Long-term effects:** app trials show fade-out (Potier Watkins & Dehaene 2024). No display aid has any follow-up beyond one session, apart from Dreschinski's months-long class use.
- **Unfinished or unpublished work:** the Lalilo RCT (France); the Kaiserslautern eye-tracking study; the SILLE study; the Erfurt word-stress project. Worth re-checking in 2027.
- **Switzerland and Austria:** very few effectiveness studies were found. Spanish and Portuguese: no colour-syllable studies beyond Lopes & Barrera.
- **Orthography:** Danish is deeper than German, Italian, Spanish or Finnish. Benefits of syllable cues in transparent orthographies may not transfer.
